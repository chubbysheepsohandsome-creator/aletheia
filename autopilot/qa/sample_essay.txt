Urban heat islands are one of the clearest ways that the shape of a city changes its own weather. On a still summer afternoon, the centre of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the farmland around it, and the difference is often larger after sunset than at midday. Dark roofs and asphalt absorb sunlight during the day and release it slowly at night, while tall buildings trap that heat and block the wind that would otherwise carry it away.

The effects are not only uncomfortable. Hotter nights mean that people who already struggle to keep cool, such as older residents and families in poorly insulated housing, get little relief from the heat. Electricity use climbs as air conditioners run longer, which in turn pushes more waste heat into the street. In some cities this loop has been linked to higher rates of hospital visits during heatwaves, although the size of the effect depends a great deal on local conditions.

Several responses have been tried. Planting street trees provides shade and cools the air through evaporation, but trees take years to grow and need water during droughts. Painting roofs white or using reflective materials can lower roof temperatures sharply, yet the benefit for people walking at street level is smaller than many advertisements suggest. Parks and water features help nearby blocks, though their cooling fades quickly with distance.

When our class measured temperatures around the school with cheap data loggers, the asphalt car park was hotter than the oval by a margin that surprised most of us, and the shaded walkway was the coolest spot we found. The numbers were rough, because the loggers were not calibrated and we could only sample a few afternoons, but the pattern matched what the research describes.

Overall, the evidence suggests that no single fix is enough. A mix of shade, lighter surfaces and better building design seems to work best, and the right mix will differ from one neighbourhood to the next. Cities that plan for heat now are likely to cope far better with the hotter summers that climate projections expect.

01 — Our Philosophy

Language contains more information than the words themselves.

Patterns, structure, consistency and linguistic characteristics can offer valuable insights into how a text may have been produced. ALETHEIA explores these signals through computational analysis.

The name comes from the ancient Greek ἀλήθεια, meaning truth, disclosure, the revealing of what is hidden. Truth is not always visible on the surface; it has to be discovered. That is also why we are careful: a signal is not a verdict, and a score is not proof.

02 — Technology

Several layers of evidence on a single text.

Trained models make the decision; the interpretable signals below show why. No single hand-picked signal decides the result on its own.

01

Linguistic analysis

Segmentation into prose, quotations, references, headings and lists, so only the author's own running prose is assessed.

02

Statistical text analysis

Sentence-length variation, paragraph uniformity, function-word ratios, punctuation habits and lexical diversity.

03

Machine-learning classification

A gradient-boosted model trained on licensed, labelled data and evaluated on held-out writers and prompts.

04

Multi-model evaluation

Benchmarks across generator families, with whole models held out to test generalisation to systems never seen in training.

05

Writing-style analysis

Template phrases, inflated significance and chatbot artefacts, weighed against signals of genuine human writing.

06

Hybrid authorship analysis

Sentence- and paragraph-level evidence that separates sections, so mixed or edited documents are shown as mixed.

07

Confidence calibration

Thresholds set on validation data so that at most 1% of human texts per writer group are flagged; low evidence is stated plainly.

—

A note on certainty

No single method, and no score, can conclusively establish who wrote a document. ALETHEIA reports patterns and their strength, never proof.

Technology

How ALETHEIA reads a text.

An independent detection engine () that combines a transparent feature extractor with a small trained classifier, all running in your browser.

Preprocessing separates qualifying prose from headings, lists, tables, mathematical lines, block quotes and reference sections. It removes quotations of six or more words, splits sentences with care for abbreviations and decimals, and checks the language.

Scoring. The engine measures 23 document features. A gradient-boosted tree model trained on licensed, labelled data turns them into a model score. Two thresholds are set on a validation split: the flagging threshold keeps false positives at or below 1% in every human writer group, and the review threshold marks the inconclusive band. Paragraph comparisons use local evidence only, so mixed documents appear mixed.

Signals, and how much we trust them

Stylistic signals draw on editorial observations such as Wikipedia's Signs of AI writing. We treat them as hypotheses to be measured, not rules.

SignalLayerRatingNotes
Chatbot artefacts (“I hope this helps”, oaicite markers)StylisticStrongRarely written by people unless quoted.
Formulaic templates (“plays a crucial role”, “not just X, it’s Y”)StylisticModerateDensity matters more than any single phrase.
AI-associated vocabulary (“delve”, “tapestry”, “underscores”)StylisticModerateDrifts as models change, so the list needs regular updates.
Sentence-length uniformity (low burstiness)StatisticalModerateCareful formal writers can be uniform too.
Human-writing signals (slips, informal words, lowercase “i”)StylisticModerateOnly ever push toward “human”; their absence is never evidence of AI.
Word length, function words, punctuation, lexical diversityStatisticalWeak aloneUsed only in combination, with per-group false-positive checks.
Perfect grammar, curly quotes, “sounds formal”—Not usedUnfair to proficient and non-native writers.

What we do not claim

ALETHEIA does not and cannot prove who wrote a document. Its scores are signal strengths on a calibrated scale, not the probability that a particular person used AI. They should never be the only basis for an academic or employment decision.

Deep Scan (deep-0.7.0, ALETHEIA V1.0)

Deep and Supreme scans combine a character- and word-level n-gram model with the standard document model. Training text comes from 120+ language models released between 2023 and 2026 (short fiction from public creative-writing benchmarks, including GPT-5.x, Claude 4–5, Gemini 2.5–4, DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen, Grok, Llama and Mistral), real ChatGPT answers (WildChat), current-model essays, news and abstracts (DACTYL), Q&A (HC3), synthetic textbooks and guides (Cosmopedia), adversarial sets (DetectRL, RAID: paraphrasing, back-translation, synonym swaps, misspellings) and human writing from public-domain fiction, Wikipedia, learner essays and news. A small genre model sorts each text into one of three registers, each with its own threshold: academic abstracts (strictest), creative writing (calibrated on human fiction) and everything else. In V1.0 the third register gets a second model trained only on non-fiction writing; the two models vote, and the combined score was calibrated again. Every threshold was set on a calibration split so that at most 1% of human texts in any group are flagged.

Compared with deep-0.4.0, V1.0 finds more AI text in every held-out group (questions and answers 1.6% → 42.7%, real ChatGPT answers 46.6% → 55.5%, current-model news 41.5% → 56.8%) and leaves fiction and academic abstracts unchanged. Two earlier candidates that retrained a single model on all genres lost ground on GPT-5.5 fiction and were not released.

For comparison, the standard 2023-era model found only 0–13% of modern AI fiction. The Standard mode (removed in V1.8) consulted the n-gram model too: it gave an AI verdict when that model clears its register's threshold, and withholds one when it disagrees. Line-numbered texts (monologues, scripts) are now read as prose; previously their lines were excluded as list items.

Weak spot: older and open models in non-fiction. Wikipedia-style articles, how-to guides and forum answers written by older or small open models (BLOOMz, Dolly, Flan-T5) are rarely detected (0–5%); ChatGPT-written ones are found 12–39% of the time. Human writing in these styles is almost never flagged (0–0.3%).

Open risk: prompt-following human writing. On Beemo, a set of answers written in 2023 by paid annotators to chatbot-style prompts, ALETHEIA V1.0 gives an AI verdict on 12% of the human stories and poems and 16% of the human rewrites (deep-0.4.0: 9% and 16%). These texts score far higher than any other human writing we have tested, so either the detector over-flags this style or some of the "human" answers were written with AI help, a known problem with crowdsourced data. Until we can tell which, treat a positive result on short writing produced to a prompt with extra caution.

What still gets through. Text run through AI "humanizers" and back-translation is detected far less often (back-translation about 30%, DIPPER paraphrasing about 50%, humanized o1-pro news articles 57%), and some highly literary single samples score in the review range rather than above the threshold. We report those as Inconclusive instead of lowering thresholds, because lowering them would flag more human writers.

V1.7.4–V1.7.7 (steps). 1.7.4: donated texts are logged with the donor's consent statement (texts without written consent from every author are never used for training; they may be used only for internal false-positive checks; labels and ages are as stated by the donor and are not verified), and a paired table records Turnitin's percentage next to ours. 1.7.5: lines that are mostly equations or formulas are shown but not scored, which removes a known false-positive source in mathematics reports. 1.7.6: measured the headline figure at each sensitivity level against Turnitin on the first paired documents (no model change). 1.7.7: cleaner options and result text. With only two paired documents so far, nothing is calibrated to Turnitin yet.

V1.7.8 detection levels. In Student assignment mode you can pick the level directly: T (strict student threshold only, fewest false alarms: 2 of 1,857 held-out human essays reach 20%), High (the default; adds amber screening leads: 22 of 1,857, 1.2%, as of V1.7; the V1.6 table below shows 20) or Very high (about 2%). We have compared T with Turnitin on only two documents so far, so we cannot yet say how closely it matches. High catches more AI text but flags more human writing: in our own checks, two self-declared human school reports (one of them scored 0% by Turnitin) reached about 30% at High and showed under 20% at T. Read amber passages as leads, and check at T before acting on a result.

V1.7: short AI passages, annotated Word files, feedback

Short AI passages. We built a labelled test set of mixed student essays: human essays with one AI paragraph inserted, an AI conclusion added, an AI body with a human opening, human and AI halves, and sentence-by-sentence interleaving. Our 250-word segments diluted short AI paragraphs almost completely. From V1.7, student-assignment mode also scores 100-word windows and highlights a sentence when either pass reaches its threshold; the thresholds were set so that highlighted words in human calibration essays rise by at most a quarter.

Interleaved single sentences remain close to chance for every model we run in the browser, and human sentences next to an AI paragraph are often highlighted too. Treat highlighted passages as places to look, never as proof.

Annotated Word files. After a Deep or Supreme scan of an uploaded .docx, you can download your own document with the flagged sentences shaded (teal, and amber for screening leads). It is built in your browser; the text, formatting, equations and images are left as they were. Sentences inside text boxes or tracked changes may not be shaded; the page and PDF always show the full result.

Feedback and donations. You can report a result you think is wrong (numbers only, never the text) or donate a text, both by email from your own email app. Feedback helps us find problems; it is never used directly as training labels.

V1.5: high-sensitivity screening, and what ensembles can and cannot add

Some users would rather check a few extra essays than miss AI text. V1.5 adds an optional High sensitivity setting inside student-assignment mode. Ensembles of the browser models. We tested unions of rules, rank-averaged voting and stacked classifiers (logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees) over every signal the browser already computes: the whole-document score, the 2023 feature model and three segment heads at 100-, 160- and 250-word windows. Trained on calibration documents and tested on the held-out essays above, the best ensemble beat a plain threshold shift by about one percentage point at the same false-positive rate. Most of the signal is already in one segment model, so V1.5 keeps the simpler, auditable threshold.

Ensembles with a transformer. Combining the student-assignment rule with an open DeBERTa-v3-large detector (MIT licence) in a stacked model did help: in cross-validation on 798 human essays and 284 hard AI essays it reached 84% of the AI essays with 0.25% of human essays flagged, against 56% for the browser rule alone. That model needs a server, so it is the basis for a future paid tier rather than part of this release.

V1.4: student-assignment mode

Turnitin's AI indicator is built for one kind of text: student submissions of at least 300 words. ALETHEIA's standard thresholds have to hold for everything people paste in, including news, reports and magazine features, and that costs recall on essays. V1.4 adds an opt-in Student assignment switch. It keeps the same models and lowers only the AI-percentage thresholds (by 1.10 logits), to the largest shift at which no group of human student essays in a calibration half had more than 1% of documents reach 20% AI. It applies only to documents with at least 300 words of prose.

For reference, Turnitin reports 84.2% document-level recall at a false-positive rate below 1% on its own test set. That is a different set of documents, so the numbers are not a head-to-head comparison.

Only for student work. On human writing of 300+ words that is not a student essay, the student thresholds flag about three times as many documents: 65 of 6,354 (1.0%) against 20 (0.3%) with standard thresholds, not counting the Beemo set. Magazine features (11 of 150) and formal reports (10 of 246) are the most affected. Leave the switch off for anything that is not a student assignment.

What it does not fix. A single AI paragraph inside human text is still rarely highlighted (about 16–28% of AI words in mixed documents, against Turnitin's reported 92% at sentence level), and DIPPER-paraphrased essays are still mostly missed. We tested shorter windows, a multi-scale stacker and seed-and-grow highlighting; none moved these numbers enough to ship. In a small essay sample, an open DeBERTa-v3-large detector (MIT licence) caught much of what ALETHEIA misses, but it is too large to run in a browser and flags many human news and magazine texts, so it is a candidate for a future server-side student mode, not part of V1.4.

V1.3: the segment model learns the newest models (seg-0.6)

V1.3 retrains the AI-percentage model with about 40,000 more documents written in 2026: answers to 100 chatbot prompts from 50 current models (GPT-6 Astra, Luna and Sol, Claude Opus 5 and Fable 5, Gemini 3.5, Kimi K3, Qwen 3.8, GLM 5.3 and others), explanations from 7 models, research-paper passages by GPT-6 Luna and Claude Opus 5.5 next to their human originals, and student essays, news, government reports and abstracts rewritten by GPT-5.4 and Gemini (OpAI-Bench). A first candidate learned that clear explanatory writing itself looks like AI and flagged more human magazine features, so it was rejected. V1.3 adds 4,600 human news articles, essays and open textbook pages published before 2022 as a counterweight. Thresholds must now also flag no calibration group more often than V1.2 did.

What V1.3 gives up. Detection of humanizer and paraphraser output fell (51% to 43%, 41% to 29%). The gains on current models came from training data that has no humanized text, and a variant weighted towards humanizers flagged more human writing, so it was not released. Found while testing. V1.2 gave 27 of 259 human government reports (10%) an AI percentage of 20% or more. V1.3 gives 0 of 259. If you scanned a formal report with V1.2, scan it again.

AI percentage and highlighted segments (seg-0.2, ALETHEIA V1.2, superseded)

Deep and Supreme scans report an AI percentage: the share of qualifying words in sections that look AI-generated, the measure Turnitin popularised. The document is cut into overlapping segments of about 250 words; a segment model scores each one, and each sentence takes the average of the segments it belongs to. Values from 1% to 19% are shown as *%, as Turnitin does, because false positives are more likely in that range. The whole-document deep score is still shown; the two answer different questions.

V1.2 retrains the segment model with 2026 material: texts from 33 generators released up to late 2026 (wasitaigenerated benchmark), the output of 12 commercial "humanizers" (AI Humanizer Benchmark, September and October 2026), and student essays at eight levels of AI involvement (GEDE). Every test below uses generators, humanizer cycles or essay prompts the model never saw.

Thresholds were calibrated on human writing (at most 1% of documents in any group reaching 20%), then raised by a small fixed margin after one human résumé crossed the line in testing.

Where we stand. On short texts from the newest frontier models, independent 2026 benchmarks report Pangram and GPTZero catching about 80% at near-zero false positives, while ALETHEIA's whole-document verdict catches about 21% and its AI percentage (V1.3) flags about a third, more on longer texts. Against humanizers no detector is reliable: on the same outputs GPTZero, Originality and Copyleaks range from 0% to 88% depending on the tool. ALETHEIA runs small statistical models in your browser and never sees your text on a server; closing the gap on frontier models needs a larger server-side model, which is planned. Until then, a low score on a short text is weak evidence of human authorship.

Earlier finding: academic abstracts

deep-0.2.0 flagged 25.7% of 1,834 human academic abstracts in an independent corpus (Mendeley Data, 2025), and the 2023 standard model flagged 11.1%. Both were withdrawn for that register; it now has its own, much stricter threshold.

How other detectors and "humanizers" work

Turnitin splits a submission into overlapping windows of a few hundred words, scores every sentence 0–1 with a fine-tuned transformer classifier built on an open-source foundation model, and averages the scores. It requires at least 300 words, shows scores of 1–19% only as an asterisk, and accepts missing up to about 15% of AI text to keep false positives below 1% on documents with more than 20% AI writing. A second model runs only on segments already flagged as AI and labels those that look AI-paraphrased (QuillBot and similar). Turnitin added "AI bypasser" detection for humanized text in 2025.

Humanizers (HumanizeAI.pro, Undetectable AI, StealthGPT and many more) rewrite AI output to change its statistics. An audit of 19 such tools found synonym swaps, sentence splitting, shifts in reading level, deliberate grammar and punctuation errors, typos, inserted personal pronouns and nonsense insertions; HumanizeAI.pro was among the most fluent. Detectors that rely on perplexity alone lose most of their accuracy on such text, while detectors trained on humanized examples recover most of it, provided human text run through the same tools is also labelled human so the detector learns the authorship signal rather than the rewriting.

Our gap. ALETHEIA runs entirely in your browser, with no transformer language model and no humanized training data from commercial tools. Closing that gap needs a server-side model and licensed humanizer data, which is on the roadmap below.

An honest note on time. A longer scan is more accurate only because it runs more analysis, not because it waits. Deep and Supreme use the same stronger model. Supreme adds 2,000 bootstrap re-analyses, three window sizes and paragraph influence analysis, which make the uncertainty estimate and the section map more reliable, then writes a PDF. On a laptop this takes seconds to about a minute; on a phone or with very long documents, longer.

Caveats. Human samples per group are small, so intervals are wide. Genres we have little human data for (personal essays, blogs, modern published fiction, student creative writing) use the conservative threshold, and their true false-positive rate is unmeasured. The Deep model was trained on research data whose upstream rights are still under review, so it must be retrained on commercially cleared data before a paid launch.

A dataset's own licence is not enough: we also check where its human text came from. Datasets built from scraped news, reviews, essays or forum posts are kept in a quarantine area until their source rights are cleared, even when the dataset itself is MIT or CC BY. Every import re-checks the licence, records checksums and versions, keeps per-record attribution, and never turns an unknown label into "human" or "AI". Human-only corpora are limited to text written before 30 November 2022.

Questions

Is 100/100 proof that AI wrote it? No. Scores describe how closely the writing resembles AI-generated text the models have seen. Human writing in some styles (formal, formulaic, prompt-following) can score high.

Why does it say "Inconclusive"? Because the evidence is not strong enough either way, or because the writing style (for example academic abstracts) is one where AI and human text are hard to tell apart. We prefer saying so to guessing.

Can AI "humanizers" fool it? Often, yes. Paraphrased and humanized AI text is detected far less reliably (see Research). ALETHEIA does undo invisible characters and look-alike letters that some tools insert.

Which languages work? English prose only, at least 80 words; 300+ words gives more reliable results.

Why do Deep Scan and Supreme results differ slightly? Supreme runs more bootstrap re-analyses and several window sizes, so its interval is estimated more precisely and can differ slightly; the AI percentage uses the same thresholds in both.
